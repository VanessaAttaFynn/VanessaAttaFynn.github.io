Research

Predicting energy before you spend it.

How much energy will it take to train a neural network — before you train it? That question started as my MSc thesis at the University of Ghana, defended in September 2026, and has grown into a line of papers with my supervisor, Prof. Kofi Sarpong Adu-Manu.

Scroll
01 The question

Why predict it at all?

Most energy figures for deep learning are measured after training has already happened. That's useful for reporting, but it doesn't help someone deciding whether an experiment is worth running in the first place.

That decision matters most where compute is limited by power, not just budget. A planned geothermal-powered AI data center in Kenya, announced in 2024, had stalled by 2026 over grid capacity — a reminder that for many researchers on the continent, energy is the real constraint. An a priori predictor gives them an estimate from the model's design alone, before a single GPU-hour is spent.

02 Paper 01 — Systematic review

Mapping what we can predict and what we can't

Paper 01

"A Priori Energy Prediction for Neural Network Training: A Systematic Review of Methods, Measurement Fidelity, and Generalizability"

Under review Sustainable Computing: Informatics and Systems

A PRISMA 2020 systematic review of methods that predict neural network training energy before training — how they're built, how reliably their ground truth is measured, and how far their results carry to new models and hardware.

01Inference, not trainingMost existing a priori predictors estimate inference energy. Very few target training.
02CNNs dominateThe literature is concentrated on convolutional networks, leaving other families thinly covered.
03Two design campsPredictors split between layer-wise decomposition and whole-architecture regression.
04Generalization is untestedFew studies check whether a predictor still holds on an architecture family it wasn't trained on.

Measurement fidelity: software power meters can underestimate wall-socket energy by 20–30% (Fischer et al.) — so the ground truth many predictors learn from is itself biased.

03 Paper 02 — Prediction framework

CAL-ENERGY

Paper 02

"CAL-ENERGY: Calibration- and Domain-Aware A Priori Prediction of Neural Network Training Energy"

Under review Sustainable Computing: Informatics and Systems
E = P × D

Instead of predicting energy directly, CAL-ENERGY predicts average power and training duration separately, then recomposes them. Splitting the two lets a known measurement-bias correction be applied to power alone — where the bias actually lives.

The model works from a family-agnostic feature set — parameter count, depth, FLOPs, batch size, epochs and a hardware-utilization proxy — so the same predictor applies to both MLPs (BUTTER-E) and CNNs (EC-NAS) without a layer-by-layer encoding.

Its second component, CAL-GATE, is an applicability check: it flags a prediction as untrustworthy when a request falls outside the model's training support, measured by nearest-neighbor distance to calibration data, or involves an unseen architecture family or hardware.

05 What's next

Better data for better predictions

In progress

A transferability dataset

Building a dataset to test CAL-ENERGY's cross-hardware and cross-architecture claims empirically. Architecture families are chosen by evidence of how they consume energy — not at random — and the shared features are the ones that drive energy most.

Planned

A data-limitations audit

An audit of public neural-network energy datasets: what they skew toward (often CO₂ estimates), and which fields they're missing that would explain how components drive energy.
